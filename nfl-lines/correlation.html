Correlation

A parlay priced by multiplying the legs is only correct when the legs are independent. This page measures the dependence instead of assuming it: first for markets within one game, then for players on the same club, then for the actual parlays the lab placed.

Prop lines on this page are proxies. There are no player-prop prices in the archive, so "over" means "over his own trailing 4-game average". These correlations are real measurements of player dependence, but they cannot price a real prop bet.

The parlays actually placed

Two legs

403 parlays placed. Observed joint hit rate +14.4% against a modelled joint probability of +30.8%.

Legs are in different games, so the dependence is only through shared week-level information and market structure. Compare observed vs modelled joint rate.

Three legs

384 parlays placed. Observed joint hit rate +8.1%.

No model joint probability is published for three legs because the legs were selected by edge size, which biases any independence calculation.

A parlay priced by multiplying marginals is only correct when phi = 0. Where phi is materially non-zero the fair price moves, and the direction is given by the sign of phi: positive dependence makes the product price too generous to the bettor; negative dependence makes it too generous to the book.

Why this matters for pricing

Take the strongest measured pair: a quarterback's passing yards and his WR1's receiving yards, φ ≈ 0.29. Multiplying the two marginal probabilities gives 0.190; the observed joint rate is 0.261. A parlay that pays as if the joint rate were the smaller number is paying too much on the winning side — which sounds generous until you notice the losing side is where the money is made.
