Signal catalogue

Sixteen candidate signals, each with its input, its availability, its leak risk and whether the official data can support it today.

The killer rule. Any signal whose input arrives after the decision cutoff is not a signal — it is a leak. That is why publication time, not just content, is part of every input definition.

A signal is a rule that maps information available before kickoff to a probability. A signal is useful only if it is (a) computable from a named source, (b) available before the decision cutoff, and (c) calibratable with n and uncertainty.

Rules for combining signals

  1. C1. Never multiply leg probabilities unless the legs are on different games, different teams and different players, and even then publish the empirical joint as the primary number.
    Independence is an assumption; the empirical joint is a measurement. When both are available, the measurement wins.
  2. C2. A parlay's price must show the hold compounding: a three-leg parlay at the same hold has roughly three times the margin of a single.
    Parlays are usually a worse deal per unit staked, and that is a computable fact rather than an opinion. The market page computes it for any stated price pair.
  3. C3. Correlation between legs must be reported with its interval, and a correlation whose interval spans zero must be printed as 'indistinguishable from zero'.
    With 8 games, almost every correlation spans zero. Saying so is the honest output.
  4. C4. A player-prop combo on one player (e.g. rushing yards over AND receptions over) is a single joint event and must be estimated from that player's joint outcomes, never from two marginals.
    Same-player legs can be strongly positive or negative depending on game script.
